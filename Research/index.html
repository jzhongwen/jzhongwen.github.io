Research

My research explores how animals respond to environmental change and how this knowledge can inform biodiversity conservation. I combine field observations, physiological experiments, mechanistic modelling, and spatial analyses to connect individual responses with population vulnerability and conservation priorities. My published work spans three complementary areas: physiological ecology, mechanistic modelling, and conservation biology.

1. Physiological ecology

Understanding animal responses to temperature and oxygen availability

I investigate how environmental conditions shape thermoregulation, metabolism, and reproduction in ectotherms, with a particular focus on lizards along elevational gradients. By combining fieldwork and experiments with physiological modelling, I examine why populations differ in their responses to climate change and how behaviour and physiological variation influence those responses.

Highlights from this work include:

  • Variation among populations. Incorporating physiological differences among elevational populations reveals contrasting responses to warming and projected range shifts, highlighting the importance of intraspecific variation in climate vulnerability assessments (Global Change Biology, 2023).
  • The importance of rest. Accounting for resting periods reveals substantial elevational variation in body temperature, cumulative digestion, and maintenance costs that assessments focused on active animals can overlook (Ecology, 2024).
  • Responses to hypoxia. Experiments show that reduced oxygen availability can alter thermal preferences and cold tolerance in montane lizards, while pregnant females can respond with higher body temperatures and earlier parturition (Biology Letters, 2021; Asian Herpetological Research, 2021).
  • An integrated view of vulnerability. In a commentary on tropical insects, we argue for assessing climate vulnerability through the combined effects of thermal physiology, behaviour, microclimate, and species interactions (The Innovation Life, 2026).

2. Mechanistic modelling

Predicting climate vulnerability across life stages

I develop and apply mechanistic approaches that link environmental conditions with animal physiology to predict the biological consequences of climate change. A central focus is translating temperature-dependent processes into opportunities for activity and embryonic development, and identifying constraints that differ across life stages.

In our Conservation Biology study, we assessed developmental traits and climate change impacts on embryos of 48 oviparous reptile species. We also compared embryonic development opportunities with adult activity constraints in five lizard species. Although vulnerable areas for both stages were concentrated in the tropics, conservation priorities based on embryos and adults overlapped poorly within these species. This work shows why climate vulnerability assessments need to account for the full life cycle.

Representative publication: Differences in climate change impacts on reptile embryos and adults — Conservation Biology, 2026.

3. Conservation biology

Identifying climate risks, refugia, and conservation priorities

I investigate where biodiversity is most exposed to environmental change and how spatial conservation planning can help safeguard vulnerable species. This work brings together species habitat maps, environmental projections, and protected-area coverage to identify future risks and gaps in protection.

Our Science study assessed sea-level rise exposure for 34,120 terrestrial vertebrate species under five socioeconomic scenarios. We projected that up to 15,324 species could experience habitat inundation by 2100 and identified potential refugia within the remaining, non-inundated habitats of affected species. More than 96% of these refugia lie outside existing protected areas, highlighting the need for early protection and international cooperation. This research connects global risk assessment with practical priorities for conserving habitats under future sea-level rise.

Representative publication: Safeguarding global terrestrial vertebrate species from future sea level rise — Science, 2026.
